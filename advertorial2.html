The Same NASA Cooling Technology That Keeps Astronauts Comfortable in Space Is Now Helping Adults Over 50 Finally Sleep Through the Night

Doctors are paying attention. So are professional athletes. Here's the simple at-home device behind a quiet revolution in how older adults sleep and why so many wish they'd found it sooner.

By Sarah Whitfield, Health Correspondent  •  Updated this week
[ HERO IMAGE A relaxed couple in their 50s/60s sleeping peacefully in a calm, well-lit bedroom. Warm, editorial tone. ]

More adults over 50 are quietly losing sleep to night sweats, temperature swings, and a mattress that simply doesn't help.

If you've found yourself waking at 2 a.m. drenched in sweat, throwing the covers off, then pulling them back on an hour later because you've gone cold, you are far from alone. In fact, sleep researchers now consider it one of the most common (and most ignored) complaints among adults aged 50 and over.

For years, the standard advice was the same: try a different mattress, lower the thermostat, buy lighter pyjamas, drink less coffee. Helpful in theory. But for most people, the problem keeps coming back because none of those fixes address what's actually happening inside the bed itself.

That's where this story takes an unexpected turn. The breakthrough that may have finally cracked the code on better sleep didn't come from the mattress industry at all. It came from NASA.

How Spacesuit Climate Technology Found Its Way Into the Bedroom

[ IMAGE NASA spacesuit alongside a modern bedroom; visual showing the link between the two climate systems. ]

The same precise climate-control system that keeps astronauts comfortable in space has been re-engineered for the home.

Inside every NASA spacesuit is one of the most precise climate-control systems ever built. It has to be. In space, a single degree of overheating can affect concentration, hydration, and performance. So engineers spent decades perfecting a way to move air gently and continuously around the body drawing heat and moisture away from the skin, and replacing it with a steady, controlled flow of cool, dry air.

That technology kept astronauts calm, dry, and focused in the most hostile environment in the universe. And for years, it never left the space program.

Then came a question that opened the door to something new:

"If we can keep astronauts perfectly comfortable in outer space, why are so many people uncomfortable in their own beds?" From the engineering team behind the technology

From the Spacesuit to the Mattress

The challenge of overheating in a spacesuit and overheating in bed turn out to be surprisingly similar. In both cases, the human body is wrapped in layers of insulating material that trap heat and moisture against the skin exactly the opposite of what's needed for comfort.

A team led by Dr. Lauren Carter, a former NASA climate-systems engineer, set out to adapt the core principle for the home. The breakthrough wasn't a "smarter mattress." It was the realisation that the bedding industry had been solving the wrong problem for decades.

Traditional mattresses even expensive "cooling" ones are designed to be lain on. They cannot draw heat or moisture out of the bed. So the team did what NASA had done all those years ago for the spacesuit: they built a quiet, low-profile unit that gently circulates a steady stream of temperature-controlled air directly into the bedding.

The result is a system that does for sleepers exactly what the spacesuit version does for astronauts keeps the body cool, dry, and at a stable temperature, all night long. No water tubes. No heated wires. No stiff pads to lie on. Just clean, gentle airflow.

Once the bed is made, you'd hardly know it was there.

Why Sleep Gets Harder After 50

It's not your imagination. Sleep specialists have long observed that, from our 50s onward, the body's ability to regulate its own temperature at night becomes less reliable. Hot flushes, night sweats, restless legs, an aching lower back, a partner who runs hot when you run cold any one of these can shave hours off a night's rest.

And the consequences add up quickly. Poor sleep has been linked to slower recovery, low mood, joint stiffness in the morning, foggy thinking during the day, and even a weaker immune response. In short: a bad night affects far more than just the next morning.

Common Sleep Complaints After 50

  • Waking up sweaty in the middle of the night
  • Cold feet that won't warm up, even under heavy blankets
  • Tossing and turning to find a comfortable temperature
  • Disagreements with a partner over the thermostat
  • Waking unrefreshed, even after 7 or 8 hours in bed

If even two or three of those sound familiar, you're in good company. A widely-cited survey found that more than half of couples disagree about the right temperature for sleeping and that disagreement alone is enough to keep one or both partners awake.

A Surprisingly Simple Solution: Move Air, Not Mattresses

[ IMAGE The BedJet device sitting discreetly beside a bed, with cool/warm air illustrated flowing under the duvet. ]

Instead of replacing the mattress, the device gently circulates cool or warm air directly into the bedding.

The invention Mark eventually launched is called the BedJet. The idea is, on paper, almost too simple: rather than adding another layer to the bed, a small, quiet unit sits beside (or just under) the bedframe and moves a steady stream of cool or warm air directly into the bedding through a thin nozzle.

The result, owners say, feels less like a gadget and more like the bed itself has finally learned how to behave. Cooling kicks in within seconds. Warmth in winter feels like fresh laundry, straight from the dryer. And, crucially for older sleepers, the gentle airflow draws moisture away from the body, so you don't wake up clammy.

There are no tubes of water, no heated wires, no stiff pads to lie on, and nothing that changes how your mattress feels. Once the bed is made, you'd struggle to tell it was there at all.
